# How It's Actually Made

Sourced from the show's DP, editors, and production interviews. Every item below has a
direct implication for our 60-second format, marked **→**.

---

## Writing: retroscripting

- Larry David writes an outline of roughly **seven pages** per half-hour episode. Plot points and beats only — **no dialogue, no jokes written**.
- Actors improvise all dialogue on the day. The term for this is **retroscripting**: rigid structure, free language.
- Larry holds the outline's beats in his head while improvising and steers the improv back onto them. That skill — improvising *toward a known landing point* — is the actual craft of the show.
- Seasons are also rigidly arced. The looseness is entirely at the sentence level.

**→ For 60 seconds, retroscripting doesn't scale.** You cannot burn eight takes discovering a line when you have sixty seconds of runtime. So we invert it: **we write the dialogue, but we write it as if it were transcribed from improv** — restarts, overlaps, imperfect grammar, no polish. `04-dialogue-grammar.md` is the ruleset for faking that convincingly. Then, on the day, we let the actors improvise around the written lines and keep whatever's better.

## Shooting: two opposed cameras

- **Two cameras rolling simultaneously** for most of the show's run (three from Season 9, when DP Patrick Alexander Stewart changed the look).
- The geometry that matters: **one camera on Larry, one over his shoulder onto whoever he's arguing with.** Both roll at once.
- Why simultaneous: the comedy is in the back-and-forth, and shooting both sides at once means the editor can **remove pauses and still have the two sides match.** If you shoot the sides separately, you can't cut the air without a continuity break.
- Cinéma vérité: handheld, available light, real locations, small crew, constant micro-reframes. The camera behaves like a documentary operator who is slightly behind the conversation.
- ~7–8 takes per scene, high for television.
- Roughly **30 hours of footage per half-hour episode.**
- Format history: 4:3 SD for six seasons, 16:9 from 2009, Sony FS7 then Panasonic VariCam LT at UHD/23.98 for later seasons.

**→** The opposed-camera pair is the single most reproducible thing about the show and accounts for most of its visual signature. Two operators, two cameras, roll both, always. Everything else about the look is free — available light and a real room are cheaper than a lit set.

## Cutting: where the comedy is actually made

The editors, not the writers, find the show. Their work is:

- Choosing from 7–8 takes of improvised material, often assembling one scene from several.
- **Cutting the air.** Removing hesitation, dead space, false starts that don't earn anything.
- Then deliberately *leaving in* the pauses that are unbearable. The show's rhythm is fast-fast-fast-**stop**.
- "Palestinian Chicken" won the Emmy for single-camera comedy picture editing. That's the correct department to give it to.

**→** Budget your silences. In a 60-second episode you can afford **exactly one** uncomfortable pause. Put it immediately after the accusation lands, before the lead's technically-correct defence. That pause is the show.

## Music: the tonal permission

- The theme is **"Frolic" by Luciano Michelini**, written in 1974 for the film *La bellissima estate*. David found it in a bank advertisement and used it four years later. Small comic ensemble — tuba, mandolin, banjo, piano, with a circus-and-bossa-nova feel.
- **There is no score under scenes.** None. Music appears only as transitional stings between scenes and over the titles.
- **There is no laugh track.**

This is the most underrated production decision in the show. The scenes are performed
and shot completely straight — no comic lighting, no comic scoring, no audience. If
you removed the music you would have a mildly harrowing drama about a difficult man.
**The bumper stings are the only thing telling the audience it's a comedy**, and
because they arrive *between* scenes, the scenes themselves are free to play at total
sincerity. That gap — straight scenes, silly connective tissue — is where the show's
tone lives.

**→ Do not use "Frolic".** It's licensed for HBO's show and controlled by Michelini's
publisher; you cannot post with it. Commission or licence an **original cue in the same
family**:

> **Music brief.** Small acoustic comic ensemble. Tuba on the bass line (on-beat, slightly
> plodding), mandolin or banjo carrying a simple stepwise melody, upright piano comping
> off-beat, brushed snare or woodblock. Major key, ~120 bpm, 4–6 bars, resolving on a clean
> cadence. Deliver three lengths: a **10-second title bed**, a **3-second sting** for
> scene transitions, and a **2-second button** with a hard final hit. No synths, no drums
> kit, nothing that sounds produced. It should sound like a small band in a room in 1974.

## Sound

- Location sound, room tone, real ambience. No sweetening, no comedy SFX, no boings.
- **→** For us: the dust extractor, the compressor kicking in, a radio in the workshop, traffic outside the roller door, the beep of a reversing truck. Real noise is characterisation.

## Performance direction

- Nobody plays for laughs. Everyone plays the stakes as real.
- The lead never plays embarrassment (see `02`, rule 8).
- Supporting players are cast for **specificity, not comic ability** — the mild man behind the counter should be an actually mild man.
- Overlap is encouraged. Let actors step on each other's last three words.

## The economics of the adaptation

| Curb | Us |
|---|---|
| 30 hours shot → 30 min | ~40 min shot → 60 sec |
| 7-page outline, no dialogue | 1-page script, dialogue written as fake improv |
| 7–8 takes per scene | 4–5 takes per scene, 3 scenes |
| 2–3 cameras | 2 cameras, non-negotiable |
| 5–6 locations | 3 maximum |
| ~8 speaking parts | 4 maximum |

A single shoot day at one workshop plus one house yields four to six episodes if you
schedule by location rather than by episode.
