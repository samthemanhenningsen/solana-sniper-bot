# Dialogue Grammar — How It's Spoken

The show's dialogue is improvised, then cut. To write dialogue that reads as
improvised, you have to imitate the *artefacts* of improvisation, not its content.
Fourteen rules.

---

### 1. Nobody tells a joke
There are no setups and no punchlines. No character is witty. The funny is
**positional** — it comes from what a person is willing to say in that room, to that
person, at that moment. If a line would work as a standalone quip, cut it.

### 2. The fight is never about the thing
It's about **whether you're allowed to bring the thing up.** The first-order dispute
(the gap, the onion, the wave) dissolves within two exchanges and is replaced by a
meta-dispute about standing, tone, and who is being unreasonable. That's where the
scene actually lives.

### 3. Repeat-back
Characters echo the other person's phrase as a flat question. It's how real people
buy time and how arguments actually escalate. *"You cut the hair." / "I cut the hair."*
Once per scene. Twice and it's a tic.

### 4. Over-precision
The lead litigates quantities, durations and sequence. Numbers. *Two minutes. Half a
bottle. Three days. Two millimetres.* Precision is his weapon and his tell, and it
makes the eventual cosmic punishment land — he's destroyed by his own unit of measure.

### 5. Restarts and self-interruption
*"I'm not— that's not— okay, hang on—"* Real speech aborts and restarts constantly.
Written dialogue almost never does. Two or three restarts per page is the difference
between "improvised" and "written."

### 6. Overlap
People talk over the last three words of the other person's sentence. Mark it in the
script (`—` at the end of the interrupted line). This is also why you shoot two
cameras simultaneously; you cannot cut overlap you didn't record on both sides.

### 7. First names as weapons
Using someone's first name mid-sentence is aggressive. *"That's not what happened,
Dave."* Save it for the turn in the argument.

### 8. Enter late, leave early
No greetings, no goodbyes, no "so as you know." Every scene begins mid-conversation
and ends before the conversation is finished. Nobody is ever introduced. The audience
works out who people are from how they're treated.

### 9. Deflation
Answer a big emotional statement with an administrative question. *"My mother died." /
"Is that why you didn't send the drawings?"* This is the show's most reliable single
move and it's also rule 7 of the comedy engine in miniature.

### 10. The stall
One beat of silence after an accusation, then a non-answer. Never a denial and never
a confession — a *reframe*. He answers the question he wished he'd been asked.

### 11. Filler is character, in small doses
*Uh, y'know, I mean, look.* Two or three per page. More than that and it reads as bad
acting rather than natural speech. "Look" at the start of a line is the lead's tell
that he's about to be condescending.

### 12. The Consequence character gets volume, not theory
The Susie-function arrives, delivers 8–12 seconds of undiluted abuse, and leaves.
Never give her an argument, a reason, or a change of mind. She is weather.

### 13. Read it aloud; cut what an actor would resist
Any line an actor would flinch at saying is a line the character wouldn't say either.
This catches almost all remaining "written-ness."

### 14. Nobody explains anything
No character explains a joke, a rule, a relationship, or a plot point that the audience
can infer. Trust the frame. If something is genuinely unclear, fix it with staging, not
with a line.

---

## Melbourne translation

The American original runs on **volume and interruption**. Melbourne tradie speech runs
on **understatement, deadpan, and the withheld reaction**. The mechanics survive; the
register changes.

**Turn the volume down on everyone except the Consequence character.** Where an American
scene escalates by getting louder, ours escalates by getting *quieter and more polite*.
The most dangerous line in an Australian argument is delivered flatly.

**Indirectness is the local weapon.** *"Yeah, no worries"* means the opposite. *"Righto"*
means the conversation is over and you've lost. *"Interesting"* means it's wrong. The
threat is never stated: *"I've got a book club on Thursday"* is how a Brighton client
tells you she's about to destroy your referral pipeline.

**Tall poppy is a gift to this format.** The cardinal Australian social sin is being
*up yourself* — self-importance, insisting on your own correctness, refusing to let
things go. That is a precise description of the Curb protagonist. The engine transfers
better here than it does almost anywhere, because his defect is already the culture's
designated crime.

**Conflict avoidance raises the stakes.** In a culture where nobody says the
uncomfortable thing, the man who says it is more transgressive, not less. Every scene
where he refuses to let something go, everyone else in frame is visibly trying to end
the conversation. That tension is free.

### Register: use
*Yeah, nah. Nah, yeah. Reckon. Fair enough. Righto. Mate* (as a warning sign, never as
affection). *How ya goin'. No dramas. She'll be right. Up himself. Doing my head in.
Carry on like a pork chop. Chuck us the— Heaps. Arvo. Servo. Smoko. Bit rough. Not
great. Yeah, it's not ideal.*

### Register: avoid
*Crikey. Fair dinkum* (dated — once a season, from the oldest character only). *G'day
mate* as a genuine greeting between mates. *Throw a shrimp on the barbie.* Anything
from the outback — this is a Thomastown factory unit and a Brighton renovation, not the
Northern Territory. **The failure mode is Crocodile Dundee. The target is dry, clipped,
and slightly bored.**

### Understatement scale
This is the tonal spine. Australians grade severity downward:

| Actual severity | What's said |
|---|---|
| Catastrophic | *"Bit of a problem."* |
| Very bad | *"Not ideal."* |
| Bad | *"Bit rough."* |
| Annoying | *"Yeah, it's a bit much."* |
| Fine | *"Yeah, good."* |
| Excellent | *"Yeah, not bad."* |

Write to this table. A character who says a catastrophe is a catastrophe is
mischaracterised.
