# The Comedy Engine

Eight mechanisms, stated as rules. If an episode isn't landing, it has broken one of
these — check them in order.

---

## 1. The premise is always an unwritten rule

Every episode begins with a social rule that genuinely exists, that everybody obeys,
and that nobody has ever written down: how long a chance encounter on the street is
supposed to last, whether a gift you've been given must be visibly used, what portion
of a shared appetiser you're entitled to.

The rule is real. Articulating it is the crime. Society runs on these rules being
*unspoken*, so the man who says them out loud is attacking the mechanism itself.

**Test:** can you state the rule in one sentence, and would a stranger in a pub nod at it?
If the rule is invented or absurd, you have a sketch, not an episode.

## 2. The protagonist is right on principle, wrong on delivery, wrong on timing

This is the load-bearing wall. His logic is consistent: *if X, then Y.* The world
doesn't run on consistency, it runs on deference to context. So he is **correct and
unbearable at the same time.**

- If he were simply wrong, we'd feel superior and watch a fool. Not funny.
- If he were simply right, it's a lecture. Not funny.
- The comedy is the gap between being right about the rule and catastrophically wrong about when to raise it.

**Calibration target:** the audience should silently agree with him roughly 60–70% of
the time. Below that, the show curdles. Above that, it becomes a grievance blog.

**This is the most common failure in imitation.** Almost every bad Curb pastiche gives
the lead a position that is simply obnoxious. Write him a position you'd defend.

## 3. Escalation by refusal to let go

Normal people drop things. He does not. Each scene he re-raises the grievance to a
**new person who has less context and more power to punish him.**

The reliable shape: stakes of the grievance shrink, consequences of the grievance grow.
By the last scene he is arguing about two millimetres in front of someone who can
end his livelihood.

## 4. The convergence

Three or four threads, planted separately, all reappearing in one location at the worst
possible moment in front of the worst possible audience. The final scene is a
**collision, not a resolution.** He doesn't win. He's exposed.

Fairness rule: every element that snaps shut on him was planted in plain sight and got
its own laugh the first time. Nothing arrives from off-screen. If the audience can't
reconstruct the trap on a rewatch, you cheated.

## 5. Cosmic justice, not moral justice

He *is* punished — but almost never for the thing he actually did wrong. He gets away
with the genuine rudeness and is destroyed by the technically-innocent act. The
universe is a bad judge with excellent comic timing.

This is what keeps the show from being nihilistic: social order is restored, just
incompetently. The audience gets the satisfaction of consequences without the
sanctimony of a lesson.

## 6. Four functions around the protagonist

Not four personalities — four *jobs*. Cast for the job.

| Function | Curb | Job |
|---|---|---|
| **The Validator** | Jeff | Agrees in private, abandons in public. Gives him permission to proceed. |
| **The Consequence** | Susie | Arrives, detonates, leaves. Never theorises. Under 15 seconds. |
| **The Escalator** | Leon | Total confidence, catastrophic tactics. Makes it worse while making it sound obvious. |
| **The Mirror** | Richard Lewis | Another neurotic. Lets the protagonist be the reasonable one for 20 seconds. |
| **The Reality Check** | Cheryl | The audience's proxy. Warm, patient, visibly running out of patience. |

Every episode needs at least one **theory scene** — two people, usually in a car,
where the rule is stated out loud. That's where the audience is recruited to his side.
It is the reason the rest works.

## 7. The taboo lever

The show habitually parks the pettiest possible grievance next to the largest possible
taboo. **The taboo is never the joke.** The joke is that his tiny bookkeeping continues
undisturbed in the presence of the enormous — a category error committed with total
sincerity.

Two conditions make this safe and funny rather than cheap:
- Nobody in the scene knows they're in a taboo. Each is locally reasonable.
- The protagonist isn't transgressing on purpose. He's a literalist who genuinely doesn't see the size difference.

If a character is *enjoying* the transgression, you've written shock, not comedy.

## 8. No shame reflex

He is never embarrassed. He is **baffled and indignant.** He does not know he is doing
cringe comedy; he thinks he's the last reasonable person in Los Angeles.

This is a performance note more than a writing note, and it's the difference between
this show and every cringe comedy that came after it. Play embarrassment and the whole
form collapses — the audience's discomfort has to be *theirs*, not shared with the
character. He must be the only person in the room who is comfortable.

---

## Failure modes, ranked by frequency

1. **The lead is just an arsehole.** His position isn't defensible. Rewrite the rule.
2. **Someone states the theme.** Cut the line. The theme lives in the object.
3. **The trap arrives from off-screen.** Go back and plant it, earlier and smaller.
4. **A character tells a joke.** No one in this world is witty. The funny is positional.
5. **The lead plays embarrassed.** Reshoot. Baffled and indignant.
6. **There's a resolution.** Delete it. Hard cut on the worst moment.
7. **The taboo is the punchline.** Rewrite so the taboo is background and the grievance is foreground.
8. **The Consequence character theorises.** She doesn't have theories. She has volume.
